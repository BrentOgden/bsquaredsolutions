If your first conversation with nearly every customer starts with “Can you give me a ballpark?”, a website estimate calculator sounds appealing. But before you pay someone to build one, try answering a simpler question: **Can two people on your team take the same customer inputs and arrive at roughly the same range?**

If they can, you may have a useful tool waiting to be built. If they cannot, a pricing example or a short request form may serve customers better. Here’s how to tell.

## Decide what the estimate should help a customer do

Imagine a Denver cleaning company that wants homeowners to decide whether a recurring clean fits their monthly budget. The useful output is a range **per visit**, along with the visit frequency and what’s included. A monthly total alone leaves someone booking every other week guessing what they’ll actually pay.

Now imagine the same business handles post-construction cleanup. Square footage tells you little about paint residue, debris, or whether contractors are still on site. In that case, asking for photos may be more useful than displaying a number.

The choice is about the service, not the company. You can offer an instant estimate for routine work and a personal review for exceptions.

## Test the pricing rules before you build the calculator

Pull ten recent estimates for one service. Set the final prices aside. Give a colleague only the details a customer could reasonably enter on a phone, and ask them to estimate each job using your proposed rules. Then compare their ranges with the original estimates.

Where they disagree, write down *why*. If stairs explain the difference and a customer can count flights, add that question. If the difference depends on a technician assessing surface damage, don’t ask a homeowner to grade it as “moderate” or “severe.” Route that case to a person.

### Choose the simplest format that passes the test

| What you found | What to put on the website |
| --- | --- |
| Most jobs fall into two or three recognizable packages | A price table with an example of who each package suits |
| A few customer-observable details reliably change the estimate | A calculator that shows a range and its assumptions |
| An inspection or professional judgment determines most of the price | An estimate request form explaining what your team needs to review |

## Show what the number includes

Suppose a visitor selects three bedrooms, two bathrooms, and cleaning every other week. A bare dollar amount leaves a big question unanswered: is that the first visit or the ongoing price?

Design the result around that distinction. For example: “Estimated range per recurring visit: [low]–[high]. Based on three bedrooms and two bathrooms. The first deep clean is estimated separately.” Follow it with a button labeled **Request a first-visit estimate**. Carry the visitor’s answers into the request so they don’t have to enter them again.

If someone picks an unsupported service, explain which detail needs review. Don’t display a zero, a made-up upper limit, or an exact-looking total that your team cannot honor.

## Give the developer a short acceptance checklist

Before launch, ask someone who didn’t build the tool to try these cases:

- **A typical job:** Does the result match the range your team approved?
- **A boundary:** At the exact point where a size band changes, does the correct rate apply?
- **An exception:** Can someone request help without first accepting an estimate that doesn’t fit?
- **A change of mind:** On a phone and with a keyboard, can visitors revise an answer and find the updated range?
- **The handoff:** Does the office receive the same inputs, assumptions, and range the customer saw?

Assign one person to update the rates. Keep a few example jobs with expected results so that person can check the calculator whenever prices change.

## Judge success by the next conversation

A completed calculation isn’t a booked job. After launch, review a sample of requests with the person who prepares final quotes. Did customers understand the range? Which jobs still needed a long explanation? Were the final quotes outside the range for the same reason each time?

Those answers tell you whether to adjust a question, narrow the calculator’s scope, or replace it with a simpler pricing page.
